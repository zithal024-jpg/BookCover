Every evening, Mara crossed the headland before the village lamps came on. She knew the path by its small details: a gate that never latched, the scent of wet stone, the bend where the sea first appeared. The old keeper's house had been empty since spring, but tonight a warm light waited in its window.

She stopped beside the gate and listened. Above the wind came a quiet sound, like someone turning a page. The door stood open by the width of a hand. Beyond it, a kettle rested on the stove and a book lay beside a cup. Mara called into the room, but nobody answered.

The book was a lighthouse journal, its cloth cover faded by salt and years. On the open page, someone had written a list of familiar names. Her grandfather's name stood first. Beneath it was a drawing of the headland and a small square marked with an arrow. The ink looked fresh enough to shine.

Mara remembered that square. It was the stone cupboard where her grandfather kept spare lamp glass. As a child, she had believed it held treasures from ships. She found its brass key hanging beside the door. Inside the cupboard were three clean lanterns, a folded map, and a note addressed simply to the next keeper.

The note contained no instructions about storms or ships. It asked its reader to bring a chair, a good story, and enough tea for a neighbor. Mara read it twice, then smiled. Her grandfather had always said that a light was useful for finding things, but even more useful for helping people find one another.

Footsteps sounded on the path outside. Mrs. Bell arrived carrying a basket under one arm and a small stool under the other. She looked surprised to see Mara, then pleased. The village had been repairing the keeper's house in secret, she explained. Tonight they had hoped to test the lamps before inviting everyone to return.

They spread the map across the table. It showed every old reading room along the coast, each marked with a little lantern. Long before the village library existed, the keepers had lent books to passing sailors. Her grandfather had wanted that custom restored, and the journal contained the names of those who had promised to help.

Mara opened the basket and found bread, apples, and four books wrapped in a towel. One was a collection of sea tales she remembered from childhood. Another held careful drawings of birds. She placed them on the empty shelf beneath the window. The room began to look less like a monument and more like somewhere to stay.

By dusk, two fishermen had joined them with another bench. The schoolteacher brought a box of books, and a child arrived with a drawing of a ship. Nobody made a speech. They found places for the chairs, filled the kettle, and cleaned the last cloudy window while the sea changed from silver to a deep, unhurried blue.

When Mara lit the three lanterns, their reflections appeared beside them in the glass. For a moment the room seemed twice as full of light. She turned the journal to a blank page and wrote the date. Then she added the names of everyone present, leaving space beneath them for anyone who might arrive tomorrow.

Mrs. Bell asked who would open the house the following evening. Mara looked toward the familiar path, now nearly hidden beyond the window. She thought of the empty hour between work and supper, and of how often she had walked past without stopping. She took the brass key from the table and said she would be glad to.

Later, as the visitors left, Mara stayed to finish one chapter. Outside, the lighthouse sent its steady beam across the water. Inside, a smaller light rested on the book in her hands. Tomorrow there would be shelves to arrange and a sign to paint. For tonight, the next keeper had only to turn the page.
